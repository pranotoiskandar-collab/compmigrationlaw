MIGRATION JURISPRUDENCE FROM THE SOUTH

Migrant domestic workers

Domestic work is one of the largest occupations for women who migrate, and for decades it was left outside labor law in most countries. The ILO Domestic Workers Convention of 2011 (C189) set the first international standard. This database brings together the international, regional, bilateral and national instruments that govern migrant domestic work, and codes the law of 34 countries of destination and 10 countries of origin on the questions those standards ask.

Version 1.3 is a working draft. The UAE, Saudi Arabia and Oman entries and Bahrain's recruitment rules are coded from the legal texts (marked P); the other cells come from secondary sources and are marked S until they are checked against the official texts.

I · AT A GLANCE

What the law provides, provision by provision

For countries of destination, each column is a provision of C189, the standard the convention sets for every domestic worker; for countries of origin, each column is a duty that international law places on the state workers leave from. Select a cell to see the note and the source.

II · SIDE BY SIDE

Compare up to four countries

Pick countries to read their rules next to each other, with the note and source for every cell.

III · BUILD YOUR OWN COMPARISON

Weigh the provisions that matter for your question

There is no default ranking. Move the sliders to give weight to the provisions your question is about, and a comparison of the destinations appears. Each provision counts 2 when the law meets the benchmark, 1 when it does so in part and 0 when it does not.

Check how stable any ranking is: 10,000 random weightings

Each weighting draws every weight uniformly between 0 and 3 (fixed seed, so the result is reproducible). A country that scores highest under most weightings does so whatever one values; a split result means the comparison depends on the weights.

IV · C189 PROVISION MAP

Which provisions of C189 are most and least reflected in national law

The map counts, for each provision, how many of the destinations meet the benchmark, meet it in part or do not provide for it.

VI · CORRIDORS

Follow the law along a migration corridor

A domestic worker who moves is governed by two legal orders and often by an agreement between them. Choose a country of origin and a country of destination to see the rules before departure, the instruments that link the two, and the rules at work.

VII · INSTRUMENTS

All instruments, with filters and search

Treaties, recommendations, interpretations by treaty bodies, regional instruments, bilateral agreements and national laws. Filter by level, region, kind, topic or binding force, or search the titles, summaries and coded notes.

VIII · TIMELINE

When the instruments were adopted

Most instruments specific to domestic work date from after 2008, when the ILO placed the subject on its agenda; national reforms cluster after C189 was adopted in 2011.

    XI · METHOD AND CITATION

    How the data was built

    Use of AI: AI is used to re-confirm that none of the data we use infringes intellectual property law, to test our labels and to edit summaries into plain language, which people then re-edit. How we use AI

    The unit of analysis is the law in force for migrant domestic workers in each jurisdiction, on paper (de jure), as of the date shown. Statutes, regulations, binding standard contracts and collective agreements are coded; practice is not. Each cell records a value, a note, the provision relied on and whether it rests on the official text (P) or on a secondary source (S). Benchmarks are the provisions of C189 for destinations and of ICRMW, CMW General Comment No. 1 and CEDAW General Recommendation No. 26 for origins. A higher value means the rule is closer to the benchmark, not that a country is a better place to work.

    Codebook: countries of destination

    Codebook: countries of origin

    • Sources: the UAE entry is coded from the official English translation of Federal Decree-Law No. 9 of 2022, Saudi Arabia from the Domestic Workers Regulation (Ministerial Decision 40676/1445), Oman from Ministerial Decision No. 574 of 2025 (Official Gazette No. 1617) and Bahrain's recruitment-fee rule from the official English text of Law No. 19 of 2006. The official legislation databases of the other countries could not be reached from our collection server, so their article numbers and positions come from secondary sources and must be checked against the official texts; the check is under way.
    • Federal and sub-national systems: the United States is coded at federal level and Canada with Ontario as the reference province; state and provincial law can be more protective.
    • Background literature (not used to code current law, which has changed since): Antoinette Vlieger, Domestic Workers in Saudi Arabia and the Emirates: A Socio-Legal Study on Conflicts (Quid Pro Books, 2013); Maysa Zahra, The Legal Framework of the Sponsorship Systems of the Gulf Cooperation Council Countries: A Comparative Examination (GLMM Explanatory Note 10/2015).
    • A blank cell means the position could not yet be determined; it is left out of the comparison rather than counted as zero.
    • Reliability: one coder applied the rules to all 658 cells. A second, independent coding against the official texts is planned, with agreement reported as Cohen's kappa.
    • Any weighted comparison depends on the weights chosen; the stability check shows how often each country scores highest across 10,000 random weightings.

    For researchers

    This comparison is built for empirical legal scholarship and socio-legal research on migration and the rights of migrant domestic workers, as well as for advocacy.

    The coded variables support statistical analysis in law: cross-national comparison, regression and event-history designs, and law and economics questions such as how coverage by labor law, recruitment fees and limits on changing employer relate to the conditions of migrant domestic workers. The data can be downloaded for replication.
