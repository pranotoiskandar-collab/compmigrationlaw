    Test it yourself

    Ask your own questions of the country data: compare groups of countries and see which pairs differ, check whether two measures move together, see which factors still matter once others are held constant, or follow how fast countries ratify a treaty. Every answer sets out the null and alternative hypotheses, the test used, the p-value and whether the null hypothesis is rejected at the significance level you choose, with a 95% interval and a plain reading. You can also take the p-value from a permutation test, which reshuffles the data thousands of times instead of assuming a random sample.

    How the scores work

    Each norm gets a binding-quality score and two relevance scores, all from 1 to 5. Its rank is binding quality multiplied by the relevance for the scope you are viewing (maximum 25). With All migration law selected, the Atlas uses migration relevance (RM). With Farmworker law only, it uses farm relevance (R), so a binding farm-specific convention outranks both a binding general treaty and a farm-specific guideline. Ties are broken by relevance, then by date. Norms are ranked separately within each tier.

    Tiers

    • Global: UN and ILO treaties, declarations, treaty-body interpretations, and global private or multi-stakeholder standards.
    • Regional: four regional systems. Europe covers Council of Europe treaties, EU law and the European Court of Human Rights. Africa covers the African Union, the OAU and ECOWAS. The Americas covers the OAS system, the Inter-American Court and MERCOSUR. ASEAN covers ASEAN instruments and the Asian-African Bangkok Principles.
    • National: statutes, regulations and leading judgments of individual states. They bind only within one country, so they are ranked among themselves.
    • Publications: reports and studies, scored by the issuer's authority (A) instead of binding quality.

    The farmworker subset contains every norm in the original Farmworker Norms Atlas, plus migration instruments with a farm relevance of 3 or more. The migration view leaves out norms whose relevance to migrants is only indirect (RM 1), such as pesticide codes.

    Country ratification scores

    Ratification lists for C97, C143 and C189 (NORMLEX, checked 11 November 2026) are shown with each norm but are not part of the score, so that the ranking stays comparable with earlier releases. Each state is scored on 13 instruments. Six are farm-specific ILO conventions, which count double: C11, C99, C110, C129, C141 and C184. Seven are farm-relevant: C169, C170, C181, C190, P29, the ICESCR and the Migrant Workers Convention (ICRMW).

    • Breadth is the weighted share of the basket in force, or ratified and awaiting entry into force, out of 19 points. Denunciations count as zero.
    • Enthusiasm looks only at the seven instruments adopted since 1989, so states that became independent late are not penalized for missing 1920s conventions. Each ratification earns half a point, plus up to half a point more for speed: full marks if ratified in the adoption year, falling to zero after 15 years.
    • Overall score = 60% breadth + 40% enthusiasm.

    How implementation is assessed

    Each instrument a country has ratified, or that binds it, is assessed against four questions: Is there a national law that gives effect to it? Does that law cover everyone the instrument protects, including farmworkers, domestic workers and migrants in irregular status? Is it enforced, judging by inspection, prosecutions and the latest comments of ILO and UN supervisory bodies? Have those bodies asked for changes that remain open?

    Implemented means yes to the first three with no major open request. Partly means a law exists but coverage or enforcement falls short. Gap means the law is missing, excludes a group the instrument protects, or is contradicted in practice. Law without ratification marks national law on a standard the state has not ratified. Recommendations are tagged with the Sustainable Development Goal targets they serve: 8.7 (forced and child labor), 8.8 (labor rights and safe work), 10.7 (safe and orderly migration) and 5 (gender equality).

    Scope and limits

    The national tier covers a selection of frameworks chosen for the size of their migrant or hired farm workforce or the influence of their model. It is a selection, not a census. Two patterns stand out. Many founding labor statutes excluded farmworkers, and much later law exists to close those gaps. National reforms also tend to follow a single visible tragedy or strike.

    The "likely drivers" column is an interpretive reading of each instrument's drafting history and political context. It does not quote the drafting records (travaux préparatoires).

    For researchers

    This atlas is built for empirical legal scholarship and socio-legal research on migration and the rights of migrant workers, as well as for advocacy.

    The coded variables support statistical analysis in law: cross-national comparison, regression and event-history designs, and law and economics questions such as how recruitment fees, wage protection and limits on changing employer affect migrant workers. The data can be downloaded for replication.
