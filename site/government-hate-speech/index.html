Government Hate Speech

When those who hold public power vilify a group of people, the law in most places has almost nothing to say. This project asks why, and what the law should do about it.

I · The project

Words spoken with the authority of the state

What government hate speech is, and why the law has so little to say about it.

A minister calls a minority “vermin.” A head of state tells a crowd that a religious community is a fifth column. A governor describes migrants as an invasion. Unless such words cross the narrow line of incitement to imminent violence or genocide, almost no legal system treats them as a wrong. This project calls the phenomenon government hate speech: the use of speech in the political arena, by a person who holds or seeks public power, to vilify, dehumanize, or incite hatred against a group defined by shared traits such as race, ethnicity, religion, nationality, gender, or sexual orientation.

The definition reaches the ministerial statement and the official social media account, and also the campaign rally and the party broadcast, because politicians and parties are the vehicles through which state power is sought and used. It does not reach criticism of policies, ideas, or the conduct of individuals.

The project’s working paper, Words of the Sovereign: The Philosophical Foundations of Government Hate Speech as a Crime, argues that the law’s silence is no accident. It traces it to three assumptions in modern legal thought: that words, unlike acts, cause no real harm; that the state needs an unfettered province of action and is restrained only for the benefit of individual citizens; and that law is an exercise of pure reason, detached from emotion and narrative. In their place the paper proposes that the state is a speaker, that it can do wrong by speaking, and that official words are acts of authority. Government hate speech is an abuse of public power, and its harm comes from the authority of the speaker, not only from the content of the words.

The paper draws its evidence deliberately from Asia, Africa, Latin America, Europe, and North America, and ends by sketching the elements of a distinct crime, the institutional answers to the problem of a state asked to prosecute itself, and replies to the strongest objections.

Working paper in draft (2026). It develops an earlier working paper, “A Case for Government Hate Speech.”

From words to violence

Statistical studies consistently find an effect. In the United States, a head of state’s tweets about Muslims predicted rises in anti-Muslim hate crimes in the following days. In Germany, anti-refugee posts by a populist party predicted violent crimes against refugees. Experiments show that inflammatory speech by a leading candidate emboldens people who already hold prejudices to act on them, especially when other leaders fail to condemn it. The relationship is one of contribution, not sole causation: official words reliably accompany violence, often precede it, and in several documented cases measurably increase it. The paper is open about the limits of this evidence: few studies isolate sitting office-holders, and much of the world collects no relevant data.

III · The record

Official words and communal violence, 1992–2024

Episodes in which official words came before communal violence, 1992 to 2024.

A selection of documented episodes in which hateful speech by officials or state-recognized leaders preceded or accompanied violence against the group targeted.

  1. Serbia · 1992

    A party leader tells a rally in Hrtkovci that the village’s Croats must leave; many do, under threat. He is later convicted of instigating deportation and persecution as crimes against humanity.

  2. Rwanda · 1992

    A senior ruling-party official calls Tutsi “cockroaches” in a speech at Kabaya, two years before the genocide.

  3. Kenya · 2007–08

    A national commission documents ethnic incitement by sitting MPs and candidates around the election. 1,133 people are killed.

  4. South Africa · 2015

    The Zulu king, whose office is state-recognized and funded, tells foreigners to “pack their bags and go home.” Attacks the next month leave at least seven dead and thousands displaced.

  5. Myanmar · 2017

    The armed forces commander describes operations in Rakhine State as finishing “the unfinished job.” More than 700,000 Rohingya flee to Bangladesh.

  6. Indonesia · 2019

    Soldiers and militia call Papuan students in Surabaya “monkeys.” Protests spread to some thirty cities, with riots in Manokwari and Sorong.

  7. India · 2020

    A junior minister leads a campaign crowd chanting that “traitors” should be shot. Riots in north-east Delhi the next month leave 53 dead, most of them Muslim.

  8. Ethiopia · 2021

    During the war in Tigray, the prime minister urges citizens to “bury” Tigrayan forces; the post is removed for inciting violence.

  9. United States · 2024

    False claims by a former president and a senator that Haitian immigrants in Springfield, Ohio, were eating pets are followed within a week by more than thirty bomb threats.

IV · Normative frameworks

Six responses, no strategy

How international, regional and national law respond today, and where each falls short.

The law reaches official speech when it becomes genocidal, and reaches hateful speech when private persons utter it. It does not reach the large territory in between.

Constitutional texts

Constitutions that mention hate speech are recent: Brazil (1988), Slovenia (1991), South Africa (1996), Venezuela (1999) and Ecuador (2008). Each uses it to narrow the individual’s freedom of expression or to direct the state to punish others. None treats the state as a possible author of hate speech.

The United States

Private speech is protected unless it is directed to and likely to produce imminent lawless action (Brandenburg). Government speech falls outside the Free Speech Clause altogether (Summum), so it is neither protected nor constrained. Courts have used officials’ hostile words only as evidence that a decision was tainted (Masterpiece Cakeshop), and looked past them in Trump v. Hawaii.

The closest case

In Féret v. Belgium (2009), the European Court of Human Rights upheld ten years’ ineligibility for office against a parliamentarian convicted of incitement. The sentence rested on a general offense; the speaker’s status justified the restriction but was not the foundation of the wrong. In Mugesera (2005), the Supreme Court of Canada upheld a deportation order based on the Kabaya speech.

V · The proposal

Elements of a proposed offense

Actor, conduct, mental element, context, exclusions and consequences of a new offense.

Offered as a starting point for doctrinal development, not as a finished statutory text.

Actor

Holders of public office at any level, members of the state apparatus, and politicians, including candidates and party members, speaking in an official capacity or in the political arena. The closer to office, the graver the offense.

Conduct

Public expression that, in context, vilifies or dehumanizes a group defined by a protected characteristic, or advocates hatred of it or its exclusion from the political community. No proof of imminent violence or resulting harm is required.

Mental element

Intent to vilify or dehumanize, or knowledge that the expression would be understood that way. Negligent or careless speech falls outside.

Context

Assessed with the Rabat factors: social and political context, the speaker’s position and authority, content and form, reach, and likelihood of contributing to discrimination, hostility, or violence. Imminence is not required.

Exclusions

Good-faith criticism of the policies or conduct of groups or their leaders, statements of fact in the course of official duties, and genuine public debate on immigration, security, or religious policy that does not vilify a group as such.

Consequences

Graduated penalties, with disqualification from public office at their center. Custodial sentences are reserved for the most serious and persistent cases.

The enforcement paradox

A crime of government hate speech names the state as perpetrator while depending on the state to enforce it, and international law, which does not rest on coercive power, compounds the problem rather than solving it. The paper argues that the answer lies in institutional design and in the expressive force of law: law persuades as well as compels.

Evidence still to gather

These sources would test the claims the cross-country data cannot reach. If you work with any of them and want to collaborate, get in touch.

  • Is the link causal? Re-run the event studies in the replication files of Yanagizawa-Drott (RTLM radio), Müller and Schwarz (social media) and Edwards and Rushin (hate crimes), with data up to 2025.
  • Who is prosecuted? Code the cases in Columbia’s Global Freedom of Expression database and the European Court of Human Rights’ HUDOC by whether the speaker was an official, a politician or a critic of the government.
  • Does hate pay? Match Manifesto Project and Global Party Survey positions on minorities to election results.
  • Harm short of violence. Track minorities’ sense of belonging and public tolerance (EU-MIDIS, World Values Survey, Afrobarometer) in years when officials speak against them.
  • Before and after, district by district. Indonesia’s SNPK violence data (1997–2014) and India’s riot records allow speech-by-speech checks below the national level.

Hate speech, voting and the protection of non-citizens

Party hate speech, anti-immigration votes and impunity in 34 democracies.

This part of the project asks whether countries whose law names non-citizens explicitly see less party hate speech and fewer votes for anti-immigration parties, and whether crimes against migrants are punished. It codes four features of domestic law in 34 democracies and sets them beside V-Dem, V-Party and OSCE ODIHR hate crime data. You can test your own questions and download the data.

For researchers

This project is built for empirical legal scholarship and socio-legal research on migration and the rights of migrant workers, as well as for advocacy.

The coded variables support statistical analysis in law: cross-national comparison, regression and event-history designs, and law and economics questions such as how recruitment fees, wage protection and limits on changing employer affect migrant workers. The data can be downloaded for replication.
