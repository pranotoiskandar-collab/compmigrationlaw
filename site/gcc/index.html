MIGRATION JURISPRUDENCE FROM THE SOUTH

Migrant workers in the Gulf states

Working paper format · Version 1.3.1 · 12 November 2026

Cite as: Institute for Migrant Rights (2026), Migrant workers in the Gulf states: migration and labor laws in comparison, version 1.3.1, https://theimr.org/gcc/. Paragraphs are numbered for pinpoint citation (for example, ¶ 5.2).

Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and Oman are home to tens of millions of migrant workers. This comparative overview brings together how each labor law applies to them, the agreements each state has made with countries of origin, who the workers are and what jobs they do. You can compare the six systems and explore the data yourself.

§ 1 · AT A GLANCE

How each labor law applies

The labor laws of the UAE, Bahrain and Oman cover farm workers employed by companies, while farm and livestock workers employed by a household or private owner fall under domestic-worker law. Saudi Arabia regulates farm workers and private herders employed by individuals in a separate 2025 regulation. Qatar excludes farming and grazing work from its labor law, except in establishments that process and market their own produce. Kuwait's law has no farm provision.

Heat bans are the summer midday bans on outdoor work as reported for 2025 and 2026; ministries set the dates each year.

§ 5 · FRAMEWORKS AND AGREEMENTS

The normative framework, by level and nature

Every instrument we could trace that binds or guides a Gulf state on migrant workers. Each one is classified by level (international, regional, interregional, bilateral) and by nature (a binding treaty, an agreement, a memorandum of understanding, a protocol or annex, a standard contract, or a declaration or dialogue). Click a cell in the map to filter the list.

A memorandum of understanding usually creates no binding legal obligation; a treaty or agreement does once in force. Binding force also depends on ratification and on each state's constitution.

National laws

The domestic legal instruments that govern migrant work in each state: constitutions, labor and domestic-worker laws, residence and sponsorship rules, wages, heat, trafficking, social security, disputes and recruitment. Each row links to the official text, the official database, or the GLMM copy, and says whom the instrument covers and whom it leaves out.

Link: text = official text; db = official database (search it for the instrument); GLMM = Gulf Labour Markets, Migration and Population copy. "Checked" means the page opened on 31 October 2026; some official sites refuse automated access, so an unchecked link may still work in your browser. Items marked [to verify] or [not verified] are open questions, not findings.

    § 9 · METHOD AND CITATION

    How the data was built

    Use of AI: AI is used to re-confirm that none of the data we use infringes intellectual property law, to test our labels and to edit summaries into plain language, which people then re-edit. How we use AI

    The unit of analysis is the state's legal framework on paper (de jure) as of the date shown; enforcement (de facto) is not measured. Each state is coded on the features below by a written rule, from the text of the law where it could be obtained and from institutional or peer-reviewed secondary sources otherwise. Every coding records its source, the date checked and whether it rests on primary text (marked P) or a secondary source (marked S).

    Limitations: English translations are unofficial and the Arabic text prevails; ministerial decisions change faster than statutes; enforcement is not measured, only the rules on paper. Instruments are classified by level and nature from their titles and published texts; a memorandum of understanding is treated as non-binding unless its text says otherwise.

    Reliability and replication

    • Coding protocol: one coder applied the rules above; a second, independent coding of all cells is planned, with agreement reported as Cohen's kappa and disagreements resolved against the primary text. Until then, cells marked S should be treated as provisional.
    • Corridor analyses treat each origin–destination pair as a case. Pairs within the same Gulf state are not independent, so the presets include state fixed effects (the Gulf state as a control); the p-values do not correct for clustering and should be read as descriptive.
    • Many tests can be run on the same data. Treat any single significant result as a lead to investigate, not a finding; the statistics engine offers a Holm correction for multiple comparisons.
    • Any weighted comparison depends on the weights chosen. The stability check under the comparison shows how often each state ranks first across 10,000 random weightings.
    • Replication files: the codebook, every coded cell with its source, the instruments list and the corridor data can be downloaded below as CSV and JSON. Each release has a version number and a dated change log.

    For researchers

    This comparison is built for empirical legal scholarship and socio-legal research on migration and the rights of migrant workers, as well as for advocacy.

    The coded variables support statistical analysis in law: cross-national comparison, regression and event-history designs, and law and economics questions such as how recruitment fees, wage protection and limits on changing employer affect migrant workers. The data can be downloaded for replication.
