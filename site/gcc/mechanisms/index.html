MIGRATION JURISPRUDENCE FROM THE SOUTH · GULF STATES

Legal mechanisms for migrant workers in the Gulf states

A migrant worker in a Gulf state has four routes to a remedy: the national labor system, national human rights and anti-trafficking bodies, the home country's embassy and agencies, and international bodies. Most individual cases are won or lost in the first route. The international routes rarely give one worker a remedy, but they can change the law for everyone.

This overview is a guide, not legal advice. Rules and deadlines change; check with the ministry, your embassy or a legal aid organization before you act. Back to the Gulf States overview

1

The national labor dispute system

In all six states a wage or contract claim starts with a complaint to the labor ministry, which tries to settle it. If that fails, the case goes to a labor court or a dispute committee. Workers excluded from the labor law, such as domestic workers and in some states farmworkers, use separate routes with fewer guarantees.

All six states run a Wage Protection System that tracks salary payments through banks. It helps prove unpaid wages, but domestic workers are often outside it.

2

National human rights and anti-trafficking bodies

Each state has a national human rights body that can receive complaints, and a national committee against human trafficking. They cannot order an employer to pay, but they can press the ministry, the police or the public prosecutor, and their reports are read by UN bodies.

  • Saudi Arabia: Human Rights Commission
  • United Arab Emirates: National Human Rights Institution (since 2021)
  • Qatar: National Human Rights Committee
  • Kuwait: Diwan of Human Rights
  • Bahrain: National Institution for Human Rights
  • Oman: Oman Human Rights Commission

A warning: if a worker leaves the employer, the employer may report them as "absconding", which can lead to detention and deportation. Contact a ministry, a human rights body or your embassy first, so there is a record that you complained.

3

Your home country

Embassies and consulates of sending countries run labor attaché offices, shelters and legal aid. For Indonesians, the embassy in Riyadh, the consulate in Jeddah, the embassies in Abu Dhabi, Doha, Kuwait City, Manama and Muscat, and the consulate in Dubai give citizen services. After returning home, workers can still bring claims, report to the Ministry for the Protection of Indonesian Migrant Workers (KP2MI), and claim BPJS Ketenagakerjaan insurance.

4

International mechanisms

Gulf states have accepted almost none of the UN procedures that let an individual complain to a treaty committee. Saudi Arabia accepts complaints to the disability rights committee (since 2008) and Qatar to the Committee against Torture (since 2012). The other four accept none. Other international routes are open to everyone, whatever the state has ratified.

MechanismWho can use itWhat it can produce

There is no Gulf-wide court or complaint body for workers. The GCC coordinates labor policy among its members but does not hear complaints.

5

What helps a case succeed

  • Keep copies or photos of your contract, passport, residence card, wage slips and bank transfers.
  • File the complaint before you leave the country. Many claims are much harder to bring from abroad.
  • Write down dates, hours worked and the names of people involved, as they happen.
  • Ask for an interpreter. Ministries and embassies can arrange one.
  • Never sign a document you do not understand, especially one that says you were paid in full.

For researchers

This comparison is built for empirical legal scholarship and socio-legal research on migration and the rights of migrant workers, as well as for advocacy.

The coded variables support statistical analysis in law: cross-national comparison, regression and event-history designs, and law and economics questions such as how recruitment fees, wage protection and limits on changing employer affect migrant workers. The data can be downloaded for replication.
