Foreword

Working without a compass

When her son’s wages stopped arriving from abroad, a mother in Cianjur went to the village head. He wanted to help. He did not know which law applied.

Going to someone you trust, rather than to a government office, is not a sign of ignorance. In fieldwork with Beth Lyon among farmworker communities in rural West Java, we found that people understand their rights through their ties to family, neighbours and the people they respect. We called this the relational self. Justice is not pictured as an abstract right that one claims alone before the state. It is pursued together, through people one trusts.

Indonesia’s migrant protection law was written with a different person in mind. Law No. 18 of 2017, and the UN Migrant Workers Convention that Indonesia ratified in 2012, imagine a worker who reads her contract, knows her rights and complains in her own name. In practice the law reaches workers only through intermediaries who know it: paralegals and legal aid lawyers, migrant workers’ organisations, district and village officials, and the staff of ministries and embassies.

Here is the trouble. Most of these intermediaries, activists and officials alike, work without a compass. They do not lack good will. They lack a picture of the whole map: what international law requires, how other countries of origin deal with the same problem, what the law of the destination says, and how courts elsewhere have decided similar cases. That knowledge is scattered across government websites, gazettes and court archives, in many languages and often behind a paywall. International databases help less than one might hope. Most are designed, funded and run from the richest countries, so they ask those countries’ questions about asylum, integration and border control. Placement-fee debt, work permits tied to one employer and the exclusion of domestic and farm workers from labour law get far less room. Hardly any were built with a village in Cianjur in mind.

This report describes what we did about it in 2026. With the help of many volunteers, most of them law students from many countries, IMR has built open legal databases that are free, downloadable and explained in plain Indonesian and English. We have done this so far without a single grant.

None of it is final. These are first editions, published early so that they can be tested and corrected, and some of our coding choices are surely open to argument. I invite officials, legal aid groups, migrant workers’ organisations, lecturers, students, and former migrant workers and their families to help us improve them. Every contribution is credited and every correction is logged in the open.

If justice for migrant workers is sought through relationships, then humane law is law that reaches those intermediaries, clear enough to be retold on the front porch.

Pranoto IskandarFounding Director, Institute for Migrant Rights
