Foreword

Making promises legible

Southeast Asia is, among other things, one of the world’s great labour markets. Few regions depend so heavily on people who cross borders to work, and few have said so much, on paper, about protecting them.

Workers from Myanmar, Cambodia and Laos keep Thailand’s farms, boats and building sites running. Indonesians and Filipinos care for households in Singapore and Malaysia. ASEAN adopted the Cebu Declaration on migrant workers in 2007 and a Consensus to give it flesh in 2017. Yet ask a simple question at any regional meeting and no one can answer it with confidence: what do the laws of the member states actually say about recruitment fees, contracts, wages, or a worker’s right to change employers? The Consensus pegs protection to “national laws, regulations and policies”. A commitment pegged to national law, without a clear record of that law, is very hard to hold anyone to.

Nor can the region simply borrow the tools that already exist. Most international databases and indexes on migration law are designed and funded in the richest countries, and they ask those countries’ questions about asylum, integration and irregular entry. They pay far less attention to what shapes migration in the South: memorandums of understanding between neighbours, work permits tied to one employer, seasonal and informal flows, and the central role of sending states.

This report describes how IMR began, in 2026, to fill that gap, for Southeast Asia and for the Global South more broadly. With the help of many volunteers, most of them law students from many countries, IMR now publishes 1,912 court decisions on migrants from 97 countries, an atlas of 111 global, regional and national norms with ratification data for 187 countries, a comparison of twelve countries of origin on nine questions, and a directory of free legal help in 37 countries. Courts in the region, from the Philippine Supreme Court to Malaysian and Thai tribunals, have already built a body of reasoning on wages, documents, detention and recruiters’ liability that few judges outside each country ever see. We want it to be seen. So far, all of this has been done without a single grant.

We should be plain about one thing. This is a first draft, not a finished register. Coverage is uneven, many entries still await checking against official texts, and some of our coding choices will rightly be questioned. We published early precisely so that officials, law faculties, labour inspectors, unions and migrant workers’ organisations could improve it. Every contribution is credited and every change is recorded in the open.

None of this needs a new treaty or a new institution. It needs the region to write down, in one place, what its laws already say. After nearly two decades of promises to migrant workers, making those promises legible is the least we can do next, and it may turn out to be the most useful.

Pranoto IskandarFounding Director, Institute for Migrant Rights

Beth LyonChair, IMR Advisory Board
