MIGRATION AND LABOR LAWS IN COMPARISON

Migrant workers in Southeast Asia

Most of Southeast Asia sends workers abroad; Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brunei also take them in, largely from their neighbors. This overview codes how the law of the four destination states applies to eight groups of migrant workers, among them plantation and farm workers, domestic workers and sea fishers, on the same ten features as our East Asia comparison. It sets them beside the emigration and protection law of seven countries of origin, maps the ASEAN, international and bilateral instruments that bear on both, and lets you explore and download the data.

I · AT A GLANCE

How the law applies to each group in the destination states

The unit of comparison is the worker group, not the state, because each state runs separate regimes for different migrants. The sharpest contrasts lie within states: in Singapore the Employment Act covers Work Permit holders but not domestic workers, and in Thailand workers under the bilateral memoranda fall under the Labour Protection Act while farm and fishing workers are governed by ministerial regulations of their own.

II · COMPARE

Build your own comparison

Choose which features matter for your question by moving the sliders. A weighted comparison appears once you set a weight. Each feature counts 2 when the protection is full, 1 when partial and 0 when absent, following the codebook in section X.

Check how stable any ranking is: 10,000 random weightings

Each weighting draws every weight uniformly between 0 and 3 (fixed seed, so the result is reproducible). A group that scores highest under most weightings does so regardless of what one values; a split result means the comparison depends on the weights.

III · INTERNATIONAL AND ASEAN LAYER

Treaties, and the regional commitments

None of the four destination states is party to the UN migrant workers convention, and only Thailand is party to the two UN covenants. Thailand has gone furthest on ILO standards for fishing, as the first Asian party to the Work in Fishing Convention (2019). At the regional level, ASEAN members adopted the Cebu Declaration in 2007 and the ASEAN Consensus on the Protection and Promotion of the Rights of Migrant Workers in 2017; both are non-binding, and the Consensus applies subject to national laws.

V · COUNTRIES OF ORIGIN

The countries of origin in the region

Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos and Timor-Leste are coded on the nine features of our Countries of Origin comparison: recruiter licensing, fees, contracts, orientation, welfare funds, officers abroad, redress at home, exit restrictions and reintegration. The data is shared with that page, so each country has one record; open a profile there for the full notes, laws and instruments. Select any cell for its rule, note and source.

Treaties most relevant to countries of origin

Indonesia, the Philippines and Timor-Leste are the only parties to the UN migrant workers convention in the region; Cambodia has signed it. The Philippines is the only party to ILO C189. Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos and Timor-Leste were added to the Countries of Origin data on 13 November 2026 and are marked as new.

VI · FRAMEWORKS AND AGREEMENTS

The normative framework, by level and nature

Instruments that bind or guide the four destination states on these workers, classified by level (international, ASEAN, measures by importing states, bilateral) and by nature. ASEAN instruments apply to all members and appear under every state. Click a cell in the map to filter the list.

A memorandum of understanding usually creates no binding legal obligation; a treaty or agreement does once in force. ASEAN declarations and the 2017 Consensus are political commitments; the ASEAN Convention against Trafficking in Persons (2015) is the only binding ASEAN treaty listed.

VII · DOMESTIC LAWS

Domestic laws, with links to the official legislation databases

The statutes, regulations and orders that govern each worker group. Links lead to the official text where a stable address exists, and otherwise to the official legislation database (Laws of Malaysia, Singapore Statutes Online, the Council of State and the Royal Gazette of Thailand, the Attorney General's Chambers of Brunei), where the title can be searched.     X · METHOD AND CITATION

    How the data was built

    Use of AI: AI is used to re-confirm that none of the data we use infringes intellectual property law, to test our labels and to edit summaries into plain language, which people then re-edit. How we use AI

    The unit of analysis is the legal regime that applies to a defined group of migrant workers, on paper (de jure), as of the date shown. Enforcement (de facto) is not coded; where reports show a gap between law and practice, the note to the cell says so. Destination groups use the ten features of the East Asia codebook: eight concern labor protection and two (a route to long-term residence and family accompaniment) concern immigration status. Origin states use the nine features of the Countries of Origin codebook. Each cell records whether it rests on the primary legal text (P) or on secondary sources and statutory citations not yet checked against the official text (S). A value that could not be established is shown as unclear and counts as zero in the weighted comparison.

    Limitations: English translations of Thai, Malay and Lao texts are unofficial and the authoritative text prevails; ministerial regulations, notifications and work pass conditions change faster than statutes; Malaysia's labor law differs between the Peninsula, Sabah and Sarawak, and the codes describe Peninsular Malaysia unless the note says otherwise. Treaty status should be confirmed in NORMLEX and the UN Treaty Collection before citation.

    Reliability and replication

    • Coding protocol: one coder applied the rules to 80 destination cells (8 groups × 10 features) and 36 new origin cells (4 countries × 9 features) on 13 November 2026. The same day every cell was checked on official legislation databases only, with ministry and agency pages cited as official guidance: 63 destination cells are now P ("partly" where part of the note rests on official guidance or our reading), 22 values were corrected, and cells that could not be checked against an official text stay S.
    • Sources: only official government, court and regional-organization sites are cited as authority. Secondary sources are named as such in the cell note.
    • Feature selection: the labor features follow the core ILO standards for fishing (C188) and domestic work (C189); the mobility, residence and family features follow the restrictions that define temporary labor migration programs in Asia (Oberoi and Sheill 2024; OHCHR 2022), as on the East Asia page.
    • Any weighted comparison depends on the weights chosen. The stability check under the comparison shows how often each group scores highest across 10,000 random weightings.
    • Replication files: the codebook, every coded cell with its note and source, treaty status, instruments, domestic laws, origin states and developments can be downloaded below as CSV and JSON. Each release carries a version number and a dated change log.
